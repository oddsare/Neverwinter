# Session Prep Help

Five ways to prep a session, from a YouTube video comparing popular prep methods. Summarized in my own words; added 2026-10-23.

> [!tip] The point of prep
> The video's main point: **the goal of prep is to make you comfortable running the game.** It isn't to build the perfect session, a story that makes total sense, or perfectly balanced fights. No method is gospel. Take what works from each and mix them.

## Pick a method
| Method | Best for | Time | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sly Flourish's eight steps | Any session; prepping so you can improvise | 15 minutes to 3 hours, depending on detail | — |
| Five Room Dungeon | Sessions with a clear shape, like a TV episode | Short: five beats | Gets railroady and predictable if every session follows it |
| 3-3-3 | Loose, improvised, unpredictable games | Short | A safety net to lean on, not something to rely on |
| DMG time-based (5, 7 or 9 steps) | Pre-written adventures; new DMs | 1, 2 or 3 hours | Feels heavy without a module |
| Your own mix | Everyone, eventually | Whatever suits you | — |

## 1. Sly Flourish's eight steps
- [ ] **Review the characters.** How do they tie into the world?
- [ ] **Create a strong start.** Make the opening memorable.
- [ ] **Outline potential scenes** for this session.
- [ ] **Define secrets and clues** to hand out as play goes on.
- [ ] **Develop fantastic locations** the players could visit.
- [ ] **Outline important NPCs** they could meet.
- [ ] **Choose relevant monsters** they could fight.
- [ ] **Select treasure and magic item rewards.**

The characters and the strong start are the only parts you fully control before the players take the game somewhere unexpected. Everything else is support for improvising, as detailed as you like.

## 2. The Five Room Dungeon
1. **Entrance or guardian.** Why is this hard to get into? Hard to find, or guarded? Story-wise, why must the PCs be the ones to go? This is your strong start.
2. **Puzzle or roleplaying challenge.** A social or exploration encounter.
3. **Trick or setback.** The PCs can't just get what they want, so they have to find another way around.
4. **Climax.** A big battle or conflict with the session's big bad. Make it challenging and rewarding.
5. **Reward, revelation or plot twist.** They get what they want, or they get pushed toward the next adventure.

- It works for a literal dungeon, a session somewhere else, or a whole campaign arc.
- The rooms can be moved around, and they don't have to fit one session. Rooms 1–2 could take one week and rooms 3–5 the next.
- There's room to improvise between the rooms.
- **Risk:** the same beats every session get predictable.

## 3. The 3-3-3 method
Come up with **three of each**:
- [ ] Locations
- [ ] Combats
- [ ] Encounters (anything that makes the players roll)
- [ ] NPCs
- [ ] Organizations
- [ ] Cool things that could happen
- [ ] Rewards

Add your own categories, or go up to five of each. You end up with about 20 ideas you can throw at the players and flesh out at the table.

## 4. The DMG method, by how much time you have
**1 hour (steps 1–5):**
1. **Focus on the story.** Read the adventure, or outline the key points so the story holds together.
2. **List the encounters** and mark each one **definite**, **possible** or **unlikely**. Definite encounters happen wherever the PCs are and whatever they're doing.
3. **Gather maps** for the definite and possible encounters. Review the monsters, stat blocks, NPCs and exploration encounters.
4. **Tie each definite encounter to the PCs.** Add things that will interest them.
5. **Skim the possible encounters.**

**2 hours (add):**
6. **Carefully review each possible encounter.**
7. **Make improv aids**, like random tables and name lists.

**3 hours (add):**
8. **Skim the unlikely encounters.**
9. **Create a new encounter aimed at one player.**

The video's host thinks ranking encounters by how likely they are is the best idea here. They'd also move steps 7 and 9 into the one-hour version. If you're building every encounter from scratch, why not aim all of them at the players?

## 5. Your own method (the host's mix)
- **List each PC's connections** to NPCs, the main villain, places and the overall story.
- **Aim for three hooks per PC:** things that make a player think "that's my character's thing." If you can't find three, make more. Give them a moment with the villain, or a recurring NPC they can build a relationship with.
- **Outline the session like a monster-of-the-week episode** (*Buffy*, *The X-Files*) inside a campaign arc you already know.
- **Improvise through the session**, always tying things back to the PC list.
- **Environments** (borrowed from *Daggerheart*): for each kind of place or event (a tavern, a dragon's lair, a road, a mountain pass, a sea voyage), keep a ready list of what can happen there and how it can affect the story.
- The host usually mixes 3-3-3 with the Five Room Dungeon.
