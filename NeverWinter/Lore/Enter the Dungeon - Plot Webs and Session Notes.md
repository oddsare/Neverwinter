# Enter the Dungeon: Plot Webs and Session Notes

Two videos from **Enter the Dungeon**, summarized 2026-10-25. Brodie uses both for this campaign: the session-note layout is the [[_Templates/Session Template|Session Template]], and the plot web is how the campaign's threads get tied together.

## Session notes
In this order:
1. **Outline:** the likely outcome, not the order of events. Shows where to spend prep time.
2. **Moments:** any situation the party can land in. Always plan the **start**. Put each **fight inside its moment** and make it stand out. End with a **stock cliffhanger** (an NPC arrives with news, or a short cutscene of something the characters wouldn't see).
3. **Character moments:** one per PC: an NPC, where, how it opens, the beats to hit.
4. **Location details:** places needed this session. Keep **one tavern** (with NPCs) and **one magic shop** (owner, name, items, prices) ready.
5. **People:** each NPC as **session level** (look, voice, stat block, what they're doing now) and **plot information**.
6. **Plotlines:** what every villain and faction is doing **right now, as if the party didn't exist**.

## Folders
World, Narrative, Session, PC and NPC. The PC folder holds the **backstories** and the **backstory prep** (plot webs and backstory secrets).

## Three sizes of arc
- **Campaign arc:** the whole campaign's story.
- **Narrative arc:** a "season," about **9–10 sessions**, which players can keep in their heads.
- **Immediate arc:** what's happening right now, a piece of the narrative arc.

## Building a plot web
1. **Design the center first** (can be done before any backstories): the **main villain**, the **central tension** and the **theme**.
2. **Break each backstory into pieces:** every name, place, group and event (a hometown, a parent, a rival gang, an attack). Note what the player says they want answered.
3. **Connect the pieces**, to each other and to the center. Fill gaps players leave (a missing surname, an unexplained motive) and ask the player before adding to their backstory.
4. **Not everything needs connecting.** Leave gaps; fill them in over the weeks. Anything can change if no session depended on it.
5. **Add backstory secrets:** what the player wrote is what the character *knows*; the DM decides what's really going on behind it.
6. **Give antagonists nuance:** a few pure villains, and some who can be redeemed.
7. **A main villain doesn't have to be fightable.** If killing them would make things worse (a head of state, say), that's an antagonistic force, not a final boss.

## Reading a plot web
- **Nodes with the most connections matter most.** Get those characters and events right, or the stories that hang off them fall apart.
- **Paths between PCs** show which players will care about each other's stories.
- **Every PC should connect back to the center**, so everyone has a reason to care about the main story.
- **Predict the route:** from where the party starts, the closest connected node is the likeliest next step. That gives a rough arc plan, which can change.
- **Start low-stakes** while a new group gels, before the heavy story. A villain can appear early in the background as a tease.
