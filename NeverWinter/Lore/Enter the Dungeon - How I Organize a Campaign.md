# Enter the Dungeon: How I Organize a Campaign

The host has run more than two dozen D&D campaigns to level 20 and uses this setup for almost all of them. He keeps everything in Google Docs; **Brodie uses this vault, with Claude, instead.** Summarized from the transcript, 2026-10-25. The sponsor segment (Describe) is left out.

## The five folders
1. **Session** (used most)
2. **World**
3. **Narrative**
4. **PC** (player characters)
5. **NPC**

**Kept outside the folders**, for quick access: the **campaign map**, the **session zero document** he uses every time, and the **campaign pitch document** he shows players.

---

## The Session folder
A **new note for every session**, made from the same template. The template's sections, in order:

### 1. Outline
- Just getting information down.
- **Not the order of events.** It's the **likely outcome** of the session, so he can picture how things might go.
- It shows **what needs the most prep time.**

### 2. Moments
- A **moment** is any situation the characters can find themselves in: breaking into a mansion, fighting a boss monster, or the start of the session.
- **Always plan the start of each session**: where the players pick up from last time.
- **Make combat encounters stand out**, so they're easy to spot while scrolling through notes mid-session.
- **Nest each encounter inside a moment.** Flow from the moment (how the encounter starts, what the situation is) straight into the encounter itself. He says this makes his campaigns run much smoother.
- **Stock cliffhanger:** always plan an ending that leaves the players wanting more. For example:
  - an NPC arrives with new information for the party, or
  - a short **cutscene** where the players see an event the characters wouldn't normally know about.

### 3. Character moments
- Moments **tailored to each player character** for this session.
- Usually between **an NPC (or a few) and one specific PC**.
- Used to **advance a backstory**, or to note an **event that triggers** if a PC interacts with the world in a certain way.
- **His example:** a PC's brother is waiting in a tavern in town. The character moment notes:
  - **where** the brother is,
  - the **opening situation** of meeting him,
  - the **beats to hit** in the conversation (say, the brother has to tell the PC their father won the lottery).

### 4. Location details
- Baseline information on places needed this session: descriptions of cities, taverns, shops, castles.
- If the party has a **home base** in a city, this gets more detailed session by session.
- He usually keeps **at least one tavern and one magic item shop** ready:
  - **Tavern:** somewhere the players can go, with NPCs inside to talk to.
  - **Magic item shop:** the **owner's name**, the **shop's name**, and a **list of magic items with prices** the players can buy.
- "This way I'm never caught off guard."

### 5. People
- Every NPC relevant to the session, on **two levels**:
  - **Session level:** who they are, what they do, what they look like, their voice, their stat block, and what they're doing this session.
  - **Plot information:** anything plot-relevant. For example, they're plotting a coup against the king, or they belong to a guild that could help the party.
- **Follower NPCs** (who travel with the party) get **their own separate notes**, because the players deal with them every session and they need more depth.

### 6. Plotlines
- A **high-level view of the plotlines** in the campaign.
- **Not just the main villain's plan:** what **each villain and faction is up to right now**, as the session happens:
  - what they **want**,
  - **where** they're acting,
  - **who** is helping them,
  - **how** they're trying to reach their goal.
- **Write it as if the party didn't exist.** What are the villains doing when the party isn't getting involved?
- If a villain's plan needs a particular party member to do something, the party member is only a **piece without agency** in that plan.

## The World folder
- **Everything about the setting and world**, probably split into many subfolders.
- All of it in one place, so it can be found during a session.
- Also where he gathers **music, maps and descriptions** for the campaign.

---

## The Narrative folder
Everything about **the story** of the campaign.

### Three sizes of arc
- **Campaign arc:** the story across the **whole campaign**. *Curse of Strahd* example: escaping Barovia.
- **Narrative arc:** like a **season** of the campaign. What happens over the next **5 to 12 sessions**, to keep it tight. He usually makes his **9 to 10 sessions**, because that's what players can keep in their heads. *Example:* getting to Strahd and meeting him for the first time.
- **Immediate arc:** what's happening **right now**, a small part of the narrative arc. *Example:* finding an NPC who last saw Strahd.

### Also kept here
- **Brainstorming** for narrative arcs.
- **Villain plots**: working out what kind of character a villain is.
- **Factions** the PCs are up against: their **interests and desires**.

---

## The PC folder
Split in two:
1. **The backstories** themselves, all in one place, so he never has to hunt for them.
2. **Backstory prep:**
   - **Plot webs** that connect all the backstories together (see the plot web notes).
   - **Backstory secrets.** What a player hands him is **what the character knows** about their past. Behind the scenes he decides **what's really going on**, or adds something more, so the backstory works in the campaign and the player gets excited to discover secrets about their own character.

---

## The NPC folder
The **major NPCs**: what they wear, what they look like, how they sound, their **goals and aspirations**, and their **stat blocks**.
