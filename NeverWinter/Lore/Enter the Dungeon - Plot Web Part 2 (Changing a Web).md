# Enter the Dungeon: Plot Web Part 2 (Changing a Web)

The sequel to [[Lore/Enter the Dungeon - Plot Web Part 1 (Building a Web)]]. What to do when a **PC dies or a player leaves**, how to **change the main villain and central tension**, and how to **use the web in session prep**. Summarized from the transcript, 2026-10-25.

**This is how Brodie builds this campaign** (2026-10-25), along with [[Lore/Enter the Dungeon - How I Organize a Campaign]].

> [!note] About the example
> The host keeps using his example web from Part 1 (Ace, Caden, Tsutsui, the Usaburo family, Orcus). **It's his example, not this campaign's.** Names are from auto-captions, so spellings may be off.

---

## The lessons at a glance
1. When a PC leaves: **add nodes, don't subtract.**
2. A new PC should feel like **someone the party hadn't met yet.**
3. **Count nodes, not words.** Aim for about 6–7 per character.
4. **Concepts make the best nodes.**
5. **Thematic connections** count: things that rhyme.
6. **Don't take your first idea.**
7. **Leave some nodes blank** on purpose.
8. **Far connects to far.**
9. **The web must change** as the campaign goes.
10. **Changing the villain** is hard: do it for a reason, through **gray areas**, with **few new nodes** and **many routes**.
11. **Use the web in prep** to seed and support what matters.

---

## Swapping out a PC

### Add, don't subtract
In his example he randomly kills **Ace** and randomly picks a new character, **Artem**.
- **Don't delete the dead PC's nodes.** The player put real time into that character, and **you still want to honor them** long after they're gone.
- You also put a lot of work into linking those nodes to everyone else. **Limit the waste:** keep them intact and build on the web.
- Some nodes **never go away** because they're tied to every backstory (in his example, the **Red Sun attack** and **James Bryce**).
- **It's one campaign, not separate backstories.** The characters are instruments in one symphony: one can have a solo without the others being forgotten.

### The new PC: Artem (his example)
His backstory, broken down:
- An **elf over 700 years old.**
- **A concept:** after seeing a lifetime of hardship, he believes the only way people can live simple, peaceful, innocent lives is through **fear**: fear of consequences, of a monster in the dark. ("Batman, if Batman were a serial killer.") He hunted criminals for centuries, trying to become an **urban legend** that would scare criminals forever.
- He wasn't magical (a rogue or fighter), knew he'd never be strong enough, and went looking for power. He found a **lost temple of an unnamed eldritch god** and became a **Great Old One warlock**.
- The process **broke his mind** for years before it came back together. His patron gave him a **younger body that doesn't age**, in exchange for tasks (about one a month). **Nothing else is known about the patron.**
- When he started taking vengeance with his new power, a **secret organization attacked him and put a mask on him** that limits his powers and his link to his patron.

That's only **five nodes**, from one of the longest backstories (Ace had six, Caden seven).

### Count nodes, not words
- **Length doesn't equal nodes.** Proper nouns, organizations and recent events make nodes.
- A player can write a short backstory that still gives the DM a lot, by writing with that in mind.

### Linking the new PC in
- **Go beyond the obvious.** The secret organization could be the Red Sun (obvious), but maybe the player wants to move past Ace. He picks the **Usaburo family** instead.
- **The mask:** from **Megahime's family**, who make magical masks, which also ties it to artifact 2 (also a mask).
- **Don't take your first idea.** "Your first instincts are usually bad." Be critical: would this connection work for **your** players, **your** game, **your** style?

### Thematic ("phantom") connections
- Beyond plot links, look for things that **rhyme**.
- **Caden lost something because of an artifact. So did Artem.** Make them rhyme: maybe these masks **divert power to whoever holds them**, and the Usaburo are using them to **power up the artifact**.
- That opens doors: **more people like Artem** with similar masks, heard about in tavern rumors or met in random encounters (one was a mage, another a barbarian), each with power being drained.
- It also gives the players a **reason to roleplay** with each other (Artem and Caden).

### Leave nodes blank on purpose
- **The eldritch patron stays blank.** Maybe it's an enemy of Orcus; maybe not. (An enemy of Orcus isn't automatically good.)
- A blank node is a **"get out of jail free" card**: if the campaign backs into a corner and a new thread needs a connection, it's there. It might even grow into a **future villain**.
- "Ideas are cheap; execution is everything." Some connections that look contrived on paper play fine at the table, and vice versa.

### Far connects to far
- "He murdered many people in the past" is hard to tie in, because it's **long ago**.
- **Tip:** look for other things that are also far away or long ago. They're "far" from the campaign, which puts them **close to each other**.
- In his example: the foreign government's records, killing members of the cult in the past, or a **conflict with Onan Grimm** (maybe he killed one of Onan's priests).
- **Talk to the player** before deciding things about their past. Artem's broken mind also leaves room to keep some of it vague.

### Concepts are the best nodes
- Ace's and Caden's backstories are built on **events** (a village burned; a birth and an ambush). Tsutsui's is mostly smaller events with a bit of concept.
- Artem's is built on a **concept**: fear and violence create peace. A concept "exists in a vacuum," so a DM can play with it in endless ways. He **encourages players to build backstories around a concept**.
- **Put the concept next to the villain:** if the main villain believes something close to Artem's philosophy, Artem is in **philosophical conflict** with them. That's a campaign-long arc for the player: *How am I different? Do I want to be? Are they right?* It doesn't go stale over 20 sessions or 200.
- **Bounce the concept off the other PCs:**
  - **Tsutsui** lives in fear, hiding and doing their duty. What would Artem think of how Tsutsui reacts to fear? Is it healthy? Has it made their life better?
  - **Caden** can't feel emotions. How does Artem deal with **a man who can't feel fear**?
- "This one concept has more juice in it than this entire backstory." This is how a campaign starts working at the level of **theme and metaphor**, the way the best DMs do.

### Reworking old nodes for the new PC
- **Old links, if the player wants them:** maybe Artem killed a Lion Rock bounty hunter, or a group of Red Sun mercenaries. (He puts these in *italics* until he's asked the player how much the new character should connect to the old one. Some players want something completely different.)
- **An introduction:** Artem is **hunting Lillian** (she's done evil), and his research brings him into contact with **Caden and the party**. That's how the new PC walks in.
- **PC-against-PC tension** (Artem and Caden both after Lillian, differently) can be fun, but **only with players you trust** and a table that's comfortable with it.
- **Changing an unrevealed secret:** "Ace is Tsutsui Prime" no longer fits. Since it **hasn't been revealed**, it can change. **Once something is revealed in play, it's fixed.** New idea: Tsutsui Prime **wears or studies one of these masks**. The party, Artem and Tsutsui chase rumors of a similar mask, and the person under it **has Tsutsui's face**.

### The goal
**"They're not new; they're just someone we haven't met yet."** The new character should feel like they were always going to join the party at some point.

New characters are honestly easy: he could have added five. **The hard part is changing the villain or central tension.**

## Changing the main villain and central tension

### When and why
- Sometimes the villain or central tension stops working as the campaign goes on ("maybe Orcus and undeath aren't the vibe anymore").
- **Never change it randomly.** It's one of the most important parts of the campaign. But **if it isn't working, change it.**
- **Listen to the players.** What do they hate? In his example, they might grow to hate Onan Grimm, then the gods, then the clerics they keep running into: anger at gods who didn't help (Ace), gods who let injustice run wild (Artem's "problem of evil"), Caden's story exposing how demigods operate.

### A central tension can be an idea
- New central tension: **organized religion in this land is doing more harm than good.**
- It **doesn't have to be a villain**, just an idea the campaign turns around. A villain to fight (Orcus) is easier, especially for new DMs. An idea is harder, and it'll need revising (first ideas aren't fully cooked).

### Choosing the new villain
- **Onan Grimm**, who's already on the web. (A brand-new villain is possible, but it means rewriting nearly everything. He's never had to do that; it's usually a course correction.)
- **Add as few new nodes as possible.** A bloated web feels contrived, especially if players don't play every week or don't take notes. He limits himself to a handful of new nodes, and keeps about **6–7 nodes per character**.
- **Four players** is the sweet spot for 5e.

### Make the new villain rhyme with every PC
- **The false flag:** the "cult of Orcus" isn't really Orcus's. **Onan is staging it** to spread fear, so people will welcome him and his church to take control of the land.
- **Artem:** Onan's "control through fear" mirrors Artem's own ideology. Artem has to justify how his fear and violence differ from Onan's. Maybe they've been fighting each other longer than Artem knows.
- **Caden:** Onan is his father (a Darth Vader figure).
- **Ace:** James figured out at the end of his life that it was **Onan, not Orcus**.
- **Tsutsui:** the family isn't working with Orcus; **Onan is setting them up**, or at least using them.
- **Lillian:** Onan is using her rage. Her father was killed by Onan, without her knowing. Now she's even more tragic.

### Careful
- **What if the player doesn't want that?** Maybe they saw Onan as just a bad dad, not a villain. Listen.
- **Don't build the campaign on one player's backstory alone.** If that character had died in session one, the whole center would be gone.
- **The DM has to be excited** about the campaign too.

### Use gray areas
- To make the new villain feel like he **was there all along**, connect him through **gray areas**: nodes the players have **touched but know little about**.
- In his example: **Glimmer** (barely known; maybe Onan wronged her), the **foreign agents**, **Lion Rock**, and the **contracts against demons** (were they really demons? were they sent by Onan?).
- **Not dark areas:** nodes nobody has touched feel like random new information, and the twist feels cheap.
- Done well, the players thought it was Orcus, and halfway through: "it was Onan all along." A great gift to give the players.

### The villain's two motivations
- **The told motivation:** what he tells people to get them on his side ("I'm trying to help, to bring peace").
- **The secret motivation:** what he keeps in his heart. In the example:
  - Onan sets up the cult and the Usaburo as **scapegoats** to take the fall.
  - **Caden was set up as a sacrifice:** a son with his emotions in a book is Onan's **excuse to attack** ("they killed my son"), a justification for conquest. Caden and Lillian were **farmed sacrifices**.
  - The real reason: **he wants to be remembered as a great hero**, but in a stagnant time with no great villain to beat, he has to manufacture one.

### Transition nodes: many routes, not one
- Plan **how the players get from "it was Orcus" to "it's Onan."**
- A new node: a **demonologist**, a wise sage who has suspected for years that the cult isn't really about Orcus, and who worked with James. **Glimmer works for them** (how is left open).
- The **eldritch patron** (the blank node) can be filled now: an enemy of **Onan's god**. Angels are bad for business. That explains the attack on Artem: he's a warlock of that god's greatest foe.
- Plus James's clues, Lillian and Caden. **Many routes to the truth, so no railroading.**

### What the new web looks like
- **Tighter**, with more connections gathered toward the center.
- **Pros and cons:** a campaign that feeds on itself, but probably moves faster, is harder to predict, and is more complex to run. Take it in stages and always know where you are.
- **More thematic rhymes**, for example **Glimmer and Miriam**: two mothers who treated their children in very different ways.
- **To simplify:** just don't introduce some nodes (if the cult never appears, the web gets much simpler).

## The web must change
- **A plot web is never finished.** Revise it from your own notes, from what happens in play, from what the players do and care about, and as your own taste grows.
- You make **many plot webs** over a long campaign: start simple, then revise and edit.
- By the end, the web lets you work with **theme and metaphor** (he cites Brennan Lee Mulligan's games: capitalism, inequality, generational trauma), not just what's physically on the table.
- **Software doesn't matter.** He used Photoshop for the video, but uses a **notebook** at home. MS Paint works. "Focus on making an effective plot web, not a pretty one."

---

## Using the web in session prep
- **Seed and support the important nodes.** The web shows which threads the campaign is heading toward (in his example, the mask and the Usaburo family). If you don't support them, the threads that matter to several players stay underdeveloped.
- **Not everything in the campaign is on the web.** Random bandit attacks, random encounters and random NPCs don't need to be. (A recurring NPC can be added if you want them to matter more.)
- **Drop small touches that support the central tension**, even in sessions about something else. *Example (religion doing harm):*
  - a passing NPC whose life a churchman ruined,
  - a town whose cleric keeps saying "help will arrive" while taking their money,
  - even while the party is really there to hunt a basilisk.
- **Plant possible nodes for later:** "a demonologist came through and looked at this recently." The players remember it when it matters.
