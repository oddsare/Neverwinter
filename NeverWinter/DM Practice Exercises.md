# DM Practice Exercises

Seven short drills for practicing DM skills between sessions, borrowed from other creative fields. From a YouTube video, summarized in my own words; added 2026-10-24. Most take 5–15 minutes. You still have to play. These train the skills in between.

> [!tip] Why they work
> - **Some limits make you more creative.** Moderate constraints help, and too much freedom leads to paralysis.
> - **Time pressure gets you past your inner critic.**
> - **Deliberate practice beats repetition.** Structured training with clear goals and quick feedback builds skill faster than just running more games and hoping.

## 1. Crazy Eights
- Pick one problem. Fold a page into eight boxes.
- Set a timer for **8 minutes** and write one solution per box. No going back, and you have to fill all eight.
- **Why:** your first ideas are just the easiest to recall, not the best. Ideas 6–8 are usually where the interesting ones are, because your brain has to reach further.
- **Use it for:** hooks, reacting to odd player choices, and giving several paths to the same discovery so it isn't a railroad.
- **The video's example:** how could the party find out the mayor secretly leads the cult? The first ideas were a letter, a witness and a confession under pressure. Idea 7 was that the mayor votes to fund the party's investigation, with a budget big enough to keep them busy but too small to let them succeed.

## 2. Constraint box
Jazz students learn to improvise by soloing with only three to five notes, or playing only on beats two and four.
- Pick a scene and **three sensory words**. Everything you describe has to tie back to one of them.
- **The video's example:** a port-city tavern using only *rust*, *whisper* and *cold*. Rusted lantern hinges, a barkeep who whispers because of a throat injury that won't heal, a couple whispering in the corner, a sailor fingering a rusted knife, and the room going cold when a certain name comes up.
- **Why:** taking options away forces you down new paths. It's the same muscle you use when players do something you never planned for.

## 3. Core image threading
In jazz, motivic development means building a whole solo out of one three- or four-note phrase: stretching it, flipping it, compressing it, and never adding anything new.
- Pick one image you like, such as **a rusted key with a broken tooth**, and develop it only by asking questions:
  - Where else might it appear in the world?
  - What's the opposite of a broken tooth?
  - If you focus only on the rust, what does it connect to?
  - What does it mean to three different characters?
- **How it differs from #2:** there you pick from a few small ingredients. Here you take one complete idea and twist it.
- **Why:** it builds patterns you can recombine quickly. When players latch onto an unexpected detail, you can make it mean something on the spot.

## 4. Status numbers
- Draw a playing card. **2 is the lowest status and ace is the highest.** Do a monologue alone, or a conversation with another DM, and show that status through **behavior only**, with no voices or accents.
- **Status isn't social class.** It's how someone carries themselves and expects to be treated. A high-status innkeeper and a low-status prince can make the best pairings.
- **High status:** holds still while speaking, chooses when to make or break eye contact, speaks in complete sentences, takes up space, and talks a little slower, because nobody's going to interrupt.
- **Low status:** fidgets, avoids eye contact, trails off mid-sentence.
- **The video's example:** the same merchant asking the party to find his brother's journal.
  - At **king** status, he walks in, looks the apparent leader in the eye, speaks in short sentences and never justifies himself, because he assumes they'll help.
  - At **four**, he's already sitting in a corner when they arrive. He half-stands, apologizes for bothering them, rambles and trails off.
  - It's the same NPC, quest and voice, but a different person.
- **Status shifts make drama.** The confident merchant gets caught in a lie and drops to a five. The nervous one learns the party plans to destroy the journal and jumps to queen.
- **Prep tip:** before a session, draw a card for each important NPC.

## 5. Decision-point drill
Researchers showed quarterbacks game footage that froze at key decision moments, and they had to pick a receiver and explain why. In six weeks they got better at reading plays, both on video and on the field.
- Put on an actual-play show. **Pause** when a player does something unexpected or confronts an NPC.
- Set a **30-second timer** and come up with **three possible DM responses**. At least one has to be something you'd normally never choose.
- Resume and compare with what the DM actually did. The point isn't guessing right. It's building a library of situations and responses to draw on at the table.
- **Trains:** pattern recognition, reading a situation before it fully develops, and predicting what comes next.

## 6. Connect the story
- Write down **five unrelated elements**: a location, an object, a character trait, a sensory detail and a short line of dialogue. The more random, the better.
- Set a timer for **8–10 minutes** and build one coherent scene that uses all five.
- **The video's example:** flooded tunnels, a brass compass, someone who can't stop apologizing, the smell of pine resin, and the line "That's not what I was promised."
  - The tunnels flood at high tide, and pine resin seals smuggled crates in them.
  - A sorcerer hires a guide to reach the crates, but it's a setup. The guide leads them to a dead end, apologizes, and shuts a heavy gate as the water rises.
  - The sorcerer finds a brass compass on a body, which shows someone hired the guide before, and says the line.
- **Why:** your brain builds stories out of random pieces on its own (the narrative fallacy). That's what makes players think you planned everything three sessions ahead.
- **At the table:** keep track of details that went nowhere, like a name said once or an object picked up and forgotten. Store them, then bring one back two or three sessions later and connect it to what's happening.

## 7. One-page design
- Ask: **what's the most important thing to communicate about this?** If it doesn't fit on one page, you probably don't understand it well enough yet.
- **The video's example:** a five-member town council in a trade dispute, where two are secretly working together, one is being blackmailed and a murder is coming. Instead of pages of scattered notes:
  - One diagram in the middle: the five members, with arrows for alliances and conflicts.
  - One line beside each: what they want and what they'll do to get it.
  - At the bottom: the three things the players are most likely to try, and a few possible consequences.
- **Removal test:** if you can take something off the page and nothing breaks, it probably shouldn't be there.
- **Why:** you keep the complexity, but you understand it well enough to react when things go off plan.

## The takeaway
DMing is a bundle of separate skills: coming up with options on demand, describing, developing motifs, reading situations, playing characters and prepping clearly. Treat them like muscles you can train away from the table. Start with one exercise.
